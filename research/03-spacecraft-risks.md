# Risks to the Spacecraft (with real cases)

| Risk | What happens | Real case |
|---|---|---|
| **Launch failure** | The rocket fails in the first minutes | **Challenger (1986):** the O-ring seals failed in the cold. Engineers advised against launching and management overruled them. 7 people died. |
| **Heat shield** | It is damaged and the vehicle burns up on return | **Columbia (2003):** a piece of foam hit the wing at launch and the orbiter broke apart on return; 7 died. **Orion (Artemis I, 2022):** the shield lost material in more than 100 spots. With a modified reentry, Artemis II (April 2026) had only 9. |
| **Tank explosion or failure** | Oxygen, power or propellant is lost | **Apollo 13 (1970):** an oxygen tank exploded and the crew came home using the lunar module as a "lifeboat". |
| **Leaks and thrusters** | The spacecraft can't maneuver well | **Starliner (2024):** 5 helium leaks and 5 thrusters failed. The capsule came back empty and the astronauts returned months later on another spacecraft. |
| **Micrometeoroids and debris** | Impacts that puncture or damage | **Webb (2022):** a micrometeoroid hit a mirror segment. **Soyuz MS-22 (2022):** a coolant leak attributed to an impact. The ISS dodges debris several times a year. |
| **Radiation on electronics** | Memory errors, resets, burned-out parts | **Europa Clipper:** carries an aluminum-zinc vault up to 9.2 mm thick. In 2024 some transistors were found to be less resistant than expected. |
| **Stuck deployments** | An antenna or panel won't open | **Webb:** 344 single-point failures, about 80% in deployment; all of them worked. **Galileo (1991):** its main antenna never opened and it had to use a small one. |
| **Gyroscopes and reaction wheels** | The spacecraft can't point | **Hubble:** has run on a single gyroscope since 2024, with less science. **Kepler (2013):** lost its second wheel and changed missions (K2). |
| **Software or unit errors** | The spacecraft does the wrong thing | **Mars Climate Orbiter (1999):** one team used pounds and another newtons, and the spacecraft was lost at Mars. **Ariane 5 (1996):** a numeric error destroyed the rocket. |
| **Fooled sensors** | The spacecraft thinks it is somewhere else | **Schiaparelli (2016, ESA):** a saturated sensor made it think it had already landed; it shut off its engines kilometers above the ground. |
| **Landing failure** | The engine fails during descent | **Beresheet (2019):** the main engine failed and it crashed on the Moon. |
| **Dust and power** | Solar panels get covered | **Opportunity (2018):** a global dust storm left it without power and ended the mission. |
| **Extreme heat** | Instruments overheat | **Parker Solar Probe:** its carbon shield is designed for ~1,370 °C (models estimate it actually reaches ~930 °C). Behind it, the instruments stay at ~30 °C. |
| **Extreme cold** | Batteries and mechanisms fail | **Dragonfly (Titan):** −179 °C. It uses a nuclear generator (MMRTG) that also provides heat. |
| **Samples and mechanisms** | Something jams | **Perseverance:** its first sample attempt (2021) came back empty because the rock crumbled, and in 2022 pebbles jammed the sample carousel. |
| **Budget cuts** | The mission is cancelled or reduced | **Mars Sample Return:** in 2026 Congress stopped supporting the original program. Perseverance's samples remain on Mars while NASA looks for another way to bring them back. |

## Lessons NASA keeps repeating
1. **Listen to engineers** (Challenger and Columbia). When an anomaly "always happens", it becomes normal and dangerous.
2. **Check units and interfaces** between teams (Mars Climate Orbiter).
3. **Test like you fly** and validate software and sensors (Schiaparelli).
4. **Have a backup** for critical parts, and when you can't, inspect them to the maximum (Webb).
5. **Safe mode and patience:** many missions were saved because the spacecraft protected itself and the team found the fix from the ground.
